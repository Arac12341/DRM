Understanding Your Circadian Rhythm

September 4, 2026 · Week 1 · About 800 words

Your body runs on a roughly 24-hour internal cycle called the circadian rhythm. It shapes when you feel alert, when you feel sleepy, your core body temperature, aspects of digestion, and the timing of dozens of hormones. The name comes from the Latin circa diem, meaning “about a day.”

The master clock

The main circadian clock is a cluster of around 20,000 neurons in the hypothalamus called the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN. On its own, with no outside time cues, the human clock tends to run slightly longer than 24 hours, so it has to be reset a little every day to stay lined up with the outside world. The strongest signal it uses for that reset is light.

A specialised set of cells in the retina — separate from the ones used for vision — measure overall brightness and send that information straight to the SCN. The SCN then acts as a conductor, keeping smaller “peripheral” clocks in organs such as the liver and gut roughly in step with the brain and with the day outside.

How light sets the time

Timing matters as much as brightness. Light in the morning tends to shift the clock earlier; light in the late evening tends to shift it later. This is why two people who sleep the same number of hours can have very different natural bedtimes: their clocks have been nudged in different directions by when they see light and darkness.

Where melatonin fits in

The SCN controls the pineal gland, a small structure deep in the brain that releases the hormone melatonin. As evening comes and light fades, melatonin output rises. It stays elevated through the night and falls back toward morning. Researchers can measure the start of that rise — the “dim-light melatonin onset” — and in most people it begins roughly two hours before their usual bedtime.

It helps to picture melatonin as a clock hand rather than an on/off switch. It is the body’s chemical way of announcing that biological night has begun, and it carries that timing message to the rest of the body. It is not a sedative in the everyday sense of the word.

The rest of the daily pattern

Melatonin is only one part of the rhythm. Core body temperature follows a curve that peaks in the early evening and reaches its lowest point in the second half of the night, an hour or two before a typical wake time. Cortisol, a hormone linked to alertness, is lowest around bedtime and climbs steeply in the hours before waking. Subjective alertness has its own shape too, including a well-documented dip in the early afternoon that is a normal part of the cycle rather than simply a reaction to lunch.

What pulls the clock out of sync

Circadian misalignment is the term for a state where your internal clock and your actual schedule disagree. Factors commonly described in the sleep-science literature include:

  • Irregular sleep and wake times, especially a wake time that moves by more than an hour or two between weekdays and weekends.
  • Bright light in the late evening, including screens held close to the face, which can push the clock later.
  • Little daylight during the day, which weakens the “morning” signal the clock depends on.
  • Travel across time zones, where the local clock changes faster than the body clock can follow.
  • Overnight or rotating shift work, which asks the body to be awake during its biological night.

When the clock and the schedule sit far apart, people commonly report difficulty falling asleep at the time they want, difficulty waking, and sleepiness during the day.

Working with the rhythm

The circadian system responds mainly to consistent daily cues. General references such as the Sleep Foundation and Mayo Clinic describe routine practices associated with a steadier rhythm: keeping a regular wake time every day of the week, getting outdoor light in the morning, and lowering indoor light in the last hour or two before bed. These are habits rather than treatments, and any effect tends to build gradually.
